The opinion

Token and crypto legal opinions, co-signed and verifiable

A token or crypto legal opinion is a licensed lawyer's signed, dated answer to a defined legal question about a token, protocol or activity, issued to the parties who may rely on it. This page explains what goes into one, how the US, Dubai, the EU and India look at tokens as of October 2026, who signs, who relies, when an opinion stops holding, and how LEXI makes it verifiable.

What a legal opinion actually is

A legal opinion gives a reasoned answer on stated facts. It says what was reviewed, what was assumed, what question it answers, and who may rely on it. A serious opinion is scoped ("do the activities described require a VASP licence under the VARA framework"), dated (it speaks as of its date), and bounded (tax and employment excluded, say). The reliance clause carries the value: without it you hold a document you cannot rely on.

Opinion practice has said this for years. The American Bar Association's Core Opinion Principles (2017) state that a closing opinion "speaks as of its date", that the giver has no obligation to update it, and that it may be relied on only by its addressee and anyone "expressly authorized to rely"11. Those two rules decide most of what follows on this page.

What a token legal opinion covers

A token legal opinion answers one question under named law, on stated facts, as of a date. Each part matters to the people relying on it.

The question and the law it covers

The opinion states the question it answers, for example which VARA issuance category an issuance falls into, or whether an offer of the token is subject to an investment contract under US federal securities law. It names the law it covers, and anything it does not name, such as tax or data protection, sits outside it.

The facts and the documents reviewed

The lawyer lists the documents reviewed and the facts given or assumed: usually the whitepaper, the tokenomics, the smart contract addresses, the sale agreements and the marketing. Reliers read this part first, because if the token no longer matches it, the conclusion no longer holds. On a LEXI credential, that document set is bound by a scope hash.

The analysis

This is the classification work. The lawyer measures the token's rights, supply, admin controls, sale history and marketing against the test each covered law applies. The next section points to where those tests stand in October 2026.

Assumptions and limits

Every opinion carries assumptions, for example that copies match originals and that signatures are genuine, and it states what it does not cover. VARA's April 2026 guidance on asset-referenced issuances asks for exactly this: the opinion must set out its assumptions, limitations and sources, and say which facts came from the issuer3. A careful reader treats these as part of the answer.

Reliance and the date

The opinion names who may rely on it and speaks as of its date11. An opinion addressed only to the issuer does not extend reliance to an exchange unless it says so, so the reliers are agreed before drafting starts. On LEXI, the reliers are a field on the credential: see named relier in the glossary.

"Utility token" is a description, and an opinion looks through it to the facts. The SEC's 2026 interpretation sorts crypto assets by their "characteristics, uses, and functions"1, and VARA's Issuance Rulebook sets a category by the nature of the asset, the rights or value it represents and the business model behind it, not by its name2.

How the US, Dubai, the EU and India look at tokens

This section is educational commentary, not legal advice, and it speaks as of 16 October 2026. Each regime asks its own question, and the same token can land differently in each one. The corridor regulation tracker lists each dated event and what it does to an opinion's scope or date.

United States: the SEC's taxonomy of 17 March 2026

On 17 March 2026 the SEC issued an interpretation (Release No. 33-11412, effective 23 March 2026) that classifies crypto assets into five categories: "digital commodities", "digital collectibles", "digital tools", "stablecoins" and "digital securities". It says the first three "are not themselves securities", while a non-security crypto asset "can be offered and sold subject to an investment contract, which is a security", and it supersedes the SEC staff's 2019 framework1. For a relier, the date matters: a US opinion issued before 17 March 2026 was written against a framework that no longer applies. US coverage on LEXI is on the roadmap.

Dubai: VARA's issuance categories, and the federal layer since 1 January 2026

In Dubai, outside the DIFC, VARA's Virtual Asset Issuance Rulebook (19 May 2025) sorts issuances into three groups. Category 1 covers fiat-referenced and asset-referenced virtual assets, plus others VARA designates, and needs a VARA licence. Category 2 is any other issuance that is not exempt: no VARA licence is required, but all placement and distribution must be carried out by a Licensed Distributor, who takes on the job of assuring that the issuer complies with the Rulebook. Exempt VAs, such as non-transferable and redeemable closed-loop assets, have no requirements before issuance. No Category 2 asset is ever deemed approved by VARA2. Since 1 January 2026 a federal layer also applies through the Capital Market Authority, the renamed Securities and Commodities Authority, under Federal Decree-Laws 32 and 33 of 20254; our briefing on the UAE CMA covers it. Licensing by activity is on the VARA compliance page. LEXI is not licensed or endorsed by VARA.

European Union: a notified white paper, and an opinion only for asset-referenced tokens

Under MiCA (Regulation (EU) 2023/1114), an offer of an ordinary crypto-asset to the public generally needs a crypto-asset white paper notified to the national regulator, with an explanation under Article 8(4) of why the token is not an e-money token or an asset-referenced token and is not outside MiCA's scope. A legal opinion is required only for asset-referenced tokens (Articles 17(1)(b)(ii) and 18(2)(e))5. The European supervisory authorities published the templates for both on 10 December 2024 (JC 2024 28, applying from 12 May 2025); the opinion template may be completed by an in-house or external legal adviser6, so LEXI's licensed-lawyer rule is stricter than the EU floor. ESMA confirmed on 17 April 2026 that the transitional period for service providers would expire across the EU on 1 July 2026, and it has7. On LEXI, EU (MiCA) coverage is V1, next up.

India: registration with FIU-IND, and a new statutory definition from 1 April 2026

Providers of notified virtual digital asset services are reporting entities under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act and must register with FIU-IND; the third revision of the registration circular (15 September 2025) says that failing to register is itself a non-compliance with the Act8, and FIU-IND's AML and CFT guidelines (updated 8 January 2026) say the obligations are activity-based and apply irrespective of physical presence in India9. From 1 April 2026 the Income-tax Act, 2025 defines "virtual digital asset" in section 2(111), extending it to any crypto-asset on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger10. Registration is a PMLA obligation, not a licence, so an India-scope opinion records the registration evidence it saw and the date it saw it. On LEXI, India (FIU-IND) coverage is V1, next up.

Who writes a token legal opinion

An opinion on a question of law is signed by a lawyer qualified in that law: US counsel for a US securities conclusion, a lawyer qualified to advise on UAE law for a VARA conclusion, and so on. VARA's Guidance on the Virtual Asset Issuance Rulebook (9 April 2026) says a legal opinion given to VARA on an asset-referenced virtual asset issuance must be prepared and signed by a duly registered practising lawyer, not the issuer's in-house counsel, and must be dated, signed and issued on the firm's letterhead3. A token that touches several jurisdictions needs one opinion per jurisdiction, or a lead opinion with local-law opinions behind it.

On LEXI, co-signers are licensed lawyers only. The protocol retains the lawyer's firm directly, as in any engagement, and the firm is paid outside LEXI. The LEXI engine, in development on a proprietary corpus of real regulatory work, prepares the first pass as work product, and that work product is never the opinion. The assigned licensed lawyer runs their own diligence and co-signs with a stake behind the signature. No AI signs anything. What the lawyer signs, who their client is and what the stake covers are on the legal professionals page.

Who relies on one

The reader of an opinion is rarely its addressee. These are the parties who open the file, and what each one is checking.

  • Exchanges and listing teams. Some require an opinion in writing: KuCoin's listing page (updated 16 December 2025) asks for a legal opinion letter issued by a reputable law firm in a jurisdiction such as the US, UK, EU, Hong Kong or Singapore13. Most run an internal legal review and keep a file: Coinbase describes a legal review of candidate assets under applicable securities laws, including an analysis under the Howey line of cases, and tells issuers to obtain their own counsel12. Either way the team asks who signed, when, on what documents, and whether it may rely.
  • Licensed Distributors in Dubai. A Category 2 issuance is placed through a Licensed Distributor, who assumes responsibility for assuring and validating that the issuer complies with the Issuance Rulebook2. An opinion with a date, a scope and a status is a document that distributor can file and re-check.
  • VASPs and their compliance teams. Before supporting an asset, and while they support it, they keep a diligence file on it. An opinion is one dated entry in that file, never the whole of it.
  • Counterparties and integration partners. Before touching your contracts or your assets, they want to know the opinion covers the version of the documents they hold.
  • Regulators, in specific cases. VARA may require, and for issuers holding reserve assets does require, a lawyer-signed opinion on an asset-referenced issuance2,3, and MiCA requires one in an application concerning an asset-referenced token5.

Requirements vary by venue and change often. Our briefing How exchanges verify a token legal opinion records what each listing team publishes, checked at source. The LEXI page for exchanges, VASPs and Licensed Distributors puts the credential in a compliance file's terms.

When an opinion stops being one a relier can rely on

An opinion letter speaks as of its date, and the lawyer who gives it has no obligation to update it for later events or legal developments11. So the relier asks whether the opinion still speaks to the facts and the law in front of them today. Three things move it off that mark.

  • The facts change. A new mint function, a contract upgrade, a new revenue share, a changed whitepaper, or a sale into a country the letter assumed was excluded. The opinion's document list no longer matches the documents you hold.
  • The law changes. The SEC replaced its 2019 framework on 17 March 20261; the EU's transitional period ended on 1 July 20267; India's statutory definition changed on 1 April 202610. An opinion dated before any of these was written against a different rulebook.
  • The reliance changes. You were not the addressee, or your entity was not expressly authorised, or the terms you accepted were for an earlier version. Practice treats later parties as entitled only to the opinion as of its original date, if at all.

Regulators have started writing the same expectation into their rules. VARA's April 2026 guidance makes the issuer responsible for keeping an asset-referenced issuance opinion current throughout VARA's review and approval process, and obliges the lawyer to issue a revised or supplemental opinion to VARA when the structure, rights or regulatory treatment materially change3. After any such change, a relier needs to know whether the opinion still holds, and a PDF cannot tell them.

The problem with opinions today

In the founding team's practice, clearing one jurisdiction the traditional way typically takes 8 to 12 weeks, where clearing a jurisdiction means obtaining a co-signed legal opinion covering it. The opinion that took that long arrives as a PDF. The exchange that asked for it cannot verify the signature on its own, cannot check whether it was withdrawn, cannot tell whether the version in the data room is the version that was signed, and cannot tell from the document whether it is authorised to rely. So counterparties re-verify by email, or accept the PDF on faith. A reliance letter patches one of those gaps, for one party, as of the original date.

The credential

A LEXI credential is a co-signed legal opinion committed on-chain. It records who signed, for which jurisdiction, as of which date, until when, under which governing law and for whom, and it binds the opinion's scope and text with two hashes. It is not a licence, a registration, or an approval by any regulator.

Each credential carries its as-of date, an expiry and a status of Live, Expired or Revoked on Base, so a relier can check whether the opinion still stands before relying on it. The opinion body stays off-chain and encrypted, readable by the named reliers. A new opinion means a new credential, never an edit to the old one. The relier's check is three steps.

  1. Status is Live, not Expired or Revoked.

    As-of date, expiry and revocation are on-chain.Does it still speak as of a date I accept?

  2. The scope and opinion hashes match the documents you hold.

    Hash the PDF and the scope text and compare them with the on-chain commitments.Is this the document the lawyer signed?

  3. Your entity is a named relier and has accepted the reliance terms.

    The credential names who may rely on it; the reliance record shows you accepted the terms.Am I expressly authorised to rely?

Questions

What does a token legal opinion commit its signer to?

To a reasoned answer, as of a date, on a defined question under named law, on stated facts and assumptions, for the parties the opinion names. Anything outside that question, that law or those facts sits outside the opinion.

Who signs the legal opinion behind a LEXI credential?

One assigned licensed lawyer, after their own diligence. The LEXI engine prepares a first pass as work product; it is never the opinion, and no AI signs anything. LEXI is not a law firm and does not give legal advice.

Can an exchange rely on an opinion that was addressed to the issuer?

Not unless the opinion says so. Opinion practice limits reliance to the addressee and anyone the opinion expressly authorises. A LEXI credential names its reliers on-chain and records each relier's acceptance of the reliance terms, so the question has a recorded answer.

How does a relier know whether an opinion is still current?

A credential shows its as-of date, expiry and status. An opinion speaks as of its date; a credential tells you whether that date still stands. A relier then checks that the scope and opinion hashes match the documents held and that its own entity is a named relier.

Does one LEXI credential cover more than one jurisdiction?

No. One credential covers one cleared jurisdiction, where cleared means a licensed lawyer has co-signed a scoped legal opinion covering it. V0 covers VARA (Dubai) on Base Sepolia testnet. The EU (MiCA) and India (FIU-IND) are V1, next up, when one opinion can span several jurisdictions and each gets its own credential.

Is a LEXI credential a licence, a registration or a regulator's approval?

No. A LEXI credential is a co-signed legal opinion committed on-chain. It is not a licence, a registration, or an approval by any regulator, and LEXI is not licensed or endorsed by VARA. V0 runs on Base Sepolia testnet; nothing is live on mainnet.

What does it cost to read a LEXI credential?

Reading the public on-chain metadata is free. Reliance access, which opens the gated opinion body and writes an on-chain reliance record, is a fixed USDC fee. Price points publish at mainnet. Customers never need $LEXI.
