The opinion

Token and crypto legal opinions, co-signed and verifiable

A token or crypto legal opinion is a licensed professional's signed, dated answer to a defined legal question about a token, protocol or activity, issued to the parties who may rely on it. This page explains what goes into one, how regulators in the US, Dubai, the EU and India look at tokens, and what LEXI adds.

What a legal opinion actually is

A legal opinion gives a reasoned answer on stated facts. It says what was reviewed, what was assumed, what question it answers, and who may rely on it. A serious opinion is scoped ("does this activity require a VASP licence under the VARA framework"), dated (it speaks as of its date), and bounded (tax and employment excluded, say). The reliance clause is the entire value: an opinion you cannot rely on is decoration.

What a token legal opinion covers

A token legal opinion answers one question under named law, on stated facts, as of a date. Each part matters to the people relying on it.

The question and the law it covers

The opinion states the question it answers, for example whether the token is a security under US federal law, or which VARA issuance category it falls into. It names the law it covers, and anything it does not name, such as tax or data protection, sits outside it.

The facts and the documents reviewed

Counsel lists the documents reviewed and the facts they were given or assumed: usually the whitepaper, the tokenomics, the smart contract addresses, the sale agreements and the marketing. Reliers read this part first, because if the token no longer matches it, the conclusion no longer holds.

The analysis

This is the classification work. The lawyer measures the token's rights, supply, admin controls, sale history and marketing against each covered law's test. The next section summarises those tests.

Assumptions and limits

Every opinion carries assumptions, for example that copies of documents match the originals and that signatures are genuine14, and it states what it does not cover. A careful reader treats these as part of the answer.

Reliance and the date

The opinion names who may rely on it and speaks as of its date14. An opinion addressed only to the issuer does not extend reliance to an exchange unless it says so, so agree the reliers before drafting starts.

How regulators look at tokens in the US, Dubai, the EU and India

This is general information, not legal advice. Each regime asks its own question, and the same token can land differently in each one.

United States: the Howey test and the SEC's 2026 interpretation

Where a token is not itself a stock, a note or another recognised security, US securities law asks whether it, or the way it was sold, is an investment contract under SEC v. W.J. Howey Co. (1946): an investment of money in a common enterprise with an expectation of profits from the efforts of others1. On 17 March 2026 the SEC issued an interpretation (Release No. 33-11412, effective 23 March 2026) that sorts crypto assets into five categories: digital commodities, digital collectibles, digital tools, stablecoins and digital securities. It says the first three are not themselves securities, while recognising that a crypto asset can still be sold subject to an investment contract, and it keeps Howey as the test. The same release supersedes the SEC staff's 2019 framework for digital assets2.

Dubai: VARA's issuance categories

In Dubai, outside the DIFC, VARA's Virtual Asset Issuance Rulebook sorts issuances into three groups3. Category 1 covers fiat-referenced and asset-referenced virtual assets, plus others VARA designates, and needs a VARA licence. Category 2 is any other issuance that is not exempt: no VARA licence is required, but all placement and distribution must be carried out by a Licensed Distributor3,4. Exempt VAs, such as non-transferable and redeemable closed-loop assets, have no requirements before issuance. VARA decides the category by looking at the asset's nature, the rights or value it represents and its business model, so the name a team gives its token does not settle it3. Exempt VAs do not need a Whitepaper or a Risk Disclosure Statement; other issuances do5. Since 1 January 2026 a federal layer also applies through the Capital Market Authority, covered in our briefing on Decree-Laws 32 and 33. Licensing is on our VARA compliance page.

European Union: MiCA

MiCA (Regulation (EU) 2023/1114) separates e-money tokens, asset-referenced tokens and all other crypto-assets. To offer one of those other crypto-assets to the public, a person generally needs a crypto-asset white paper notified to the national regulator, and the notification explains why the token is not an e-money token or an asset-referenced token and is not outside MiCA's scope (Article 8(4)). An application to offer an asset-referenced token must include a legal opinion that the token is neither outside MiCA's scope nor an e-money token (Article 18)8. On LEXI, EU (MiCA) coverage is part of V1, next up.

India: virtual digital assets

India's Income Tax Department describes virtual digital assets (VDAs) as including crypto assets, NFTs and other digital assets, with exclusions such as Indian and foreign currency, central bank digital currency and notified digital assets. Income from transferring a VDA is taxed at 30% plus surcharge and cess9. Separately, since 7 March 2023, VDA service providers have been reporting entities under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act and must register with FIU-IND10. Neither rule decides whether a token is also a security or another regulated product in India; that is a separate analysis. On LEXI, India (FIU-IND) coverage is part of V1, next up.

Utility token legal opinions

"Utility token" is a description, and regulators look through it to the facts. MiCA defines the term as a crypto-asset only intended to provide access to a good or a service supplied by its issuer. Its public-offer rules do not apply to an offer of a utility token that gives access to a good or service that already exists or is in operation (Article 4(3)(c)), but that exemption falls away once the offeror says it intends to seek admission to trading (Article 4(4))8. VARA's issuance categories do not turn on the word "utility" at all3, and in the US the question stays whether the token or its sale is an investment contract1,2.

So a utility token opinion usually has to show that the utility exists today and that holders get access rather than economic rights. It also has to show that the sale and the marketing did not promise profits from the team's work.

Legal opinions for ICOs, IEOs, IDOs and STOs

The launch format changes who relies on the opinion and what it has to cover.

ICO or direct token sale

The issuer sells to the public, so the opinion deals with the offer itself: who could buy, from where, on what terms, and what the marketing said. In the EU a public offer generally needs a notified white paper8, and in Dubai a Category 2 issuance must be placed through a Licensed Distributor4.

IEO or launchpad sale

An exchange or launchpad runs the sale, which makes it a natural party to name as a relier. Agree its role and its questions before the opinion is scoped.

IDO

A sale through a decentralised exchange has no single venue to name as a relier, so the analysis tends to concentrate on distribution, geographic restrictions and marketing.

STO

A security token is a security by design, a category the SEC's 2026 interpretation calls digital securities2. The opinion works through how the offering meets securities rules in each place it is sold.

Who writes a token legal opinion

An opinion on a question of law is normally signed by a lawyer qualified in that law: US counsel for a US securities conclusion, a lawyer qualified to advise on UAE law for a VARA conclusion, and so on. VARA's April 2026 guidance on asset-referenced virtual asset issuances says legal opinions provided to VARA must be prepared and signed by a duly registered practising lawyer, not the issuer's in-house counsel, and must be dated, signed and issued on official letterhead6. A token that touches several jurisdictions needs one opinion per jurisdiction, or a lead opinion with local-law opinions behind it.

Who asks for one

  • Exchanges, before listing a token, usually asking whether it is a security in named jurisdictions.
  • Banks and payment partners, before opening accounts for a crypto business.
  • Counterparties and integration partners, before touching your contracts or your assets.
  • Investors, during diligence, to check that the structure holds where the users are.
  • Regulators, in specific cases. VARA requires issuers licensed to issue asset-referenced virtual assets to give it a legal opinion confirming that their reserve assets are legally segregated and protected from creditors, including on insolvency6,7, and MiCA requires one in an application to offer an asset-referenced token8.

Requirements vary by venue and change often. Coinbase, for example, says it runs its own Howey analysis on crypto assets it considers for listing, and strongly recommends that issuers seek their own qualified legal counsel11. Check the venue's current listing terms before you scope the opinion.

What to prepare before you ask for one

  • The issuing entity's incorporation documents, and who controls it.
  • The whitepaper, website, docs and every public statement about the token, including posts and AMAs.
  • Tokenomics: supply, allocations, vesting and lockups, and any buyback, burn or revenue link.
  • Smart contract addresses, audit reports, and who holds admin powers such as mint, pause, upgrade or blacklist.
  • The sale history: SAFTs, private rounds, airdrops, and where buyers were located.
  • The distribution plan: target jurisdictions, geo-blocking, and any exchange or launchpad involved.
  • The exact question your exchange, bank or partner asked, in their words.

When an opinion goes stale

An opinion letter speaks as of its date, and under the ABA's legal opinion principles the lawyer who gives it has no obligation to update it for later events or legal developments14. Token facts move quickly: a new mint function, a contract upgrade, a new revenue share, or a sale into a country the letter assumed was excluded. The law moves too, as the SEC's March 2026 interpretation showed2. After any of these, a relier needs to know whether the opinion still holds, and a PDF cannot tell them.

The problem with opinions today

The opinion that took months and real money arrives as a PDF. The exchange that asked for it cannot verify the signature on its own, cannot check whether it was revoked, and cannot tell whether the version in the data room is the version that was signed. So counterparties re-verify by email, or accept it on faith.

Frequently asked questions

What is a token legal opinion?

A token legal opinion is a licensed professional's signed, dated letter answering a defined question about a token, usually whether it is a security or another regulated instrument in named jurisdictions. It rests on stated facts and assumptions and names who may rely on it.

Is a utility token automatically not a security?

No. Regulators look at what the token does and how it was sold, whatever it is called. MiCA defines utility tokens narrowly, and in the US a token can still be sold as part of an investment contract under the Howey test.

Does a legal opinion get a token listed?

No. The exchange makes its own decision, often after its own legal review, and an opinion is one document in that file. Coinbase, for example, says it runs its own Howey analysis on crypto assets it considers for listing.

Who can write a token legal opinion?

Normally a lawyer qualified in the law the opinion covers, and VARA's guidance says opinions it receives on asset-referenced virtual assets must come from a registered practising lawyer, not in-house counsel.
