How exchanges verify a token legal opinion

What listing teams publish about legal review, what makes a PDF opinion relied on, what a VARA-licensed VASP keeps on file, and what changes when the opinion is a credential. Written for the people who read opinions, not the people who commission them.

By Lex Arbitrage (opens in a new tab), Co-Founder and CEO · Published 2026-10-11 · Last updated 2026-10-16

What a PDF can tell you

What a listing team can check in a PDF opinion.

In one passage

A token legal opinion is a PDF. A listing team can check that it is signed, dated, on letterhead and addressed to someone, and that the white paper it refers to is attached. It cannot check, without asking the law firm, whether the opinion is still current, whether the document set has changed since signing, or whether the exchange itself is authorised to rely on it. A LEXI credential records those three facts on Base as a status, two hash commitments and a named-relier list, so the check takes one call. V0 runs on Base Sepolia testnet; nothing is live on mainnet.

Four questions sit under every opinion that lands in a listing file, and the PDF answers none of them on its own.

  • Reliance. Opinion practice limits reliance to the addressee and anyone the firm expressly authorises. If the exchange is not named, it asks the firm for a reliance letter, and the firm may decline, or grant it only as of the original date.
  • Staleness. An opinion speaks as of its date, and the giver has no duty to update it. Nothing in the file says whether the facts or the law moved after signing.
  • Bring-down. A bring-down letter re-confirms an earlier opinion to a later date. It is another PDF, with the same limits, and it restarts the same checks.
  • Bar checks. The file shows a name and a firm. Checking the licence means finding the right register, which differs by jurisdiction and is sometimes not public at all.

This briefing is written for reliers. It is not a guide to getting listed, and LEXI never sits in a listing decision; it tells you what the opinion covers, who signed it and whether it is still live.

The one written requirement

What the one written requirement checks.

KuCoin's Guidelines on the Legal Opinion Required for Projects to Launch on KuCoin are undated on their face. The PDF's metadata is dated 7 May 2020, and the file is still linked from the listing page updated 16 December 2025. Whether its firm-tier, headcount and founding-date bars are applied in 2026 is not published, and nothing here suggests a LEXI co-signing firm would meet them. What the guidelines are useful for is different: they are a written list of what a listing team checks once a PDF opinion lands. Five of the articles map to a field or a flow on the credential.

Art. 7 · form, signature, letterhead, seal, date of issue
The signatory and the firm ID are public on-chain fields, and the as-of date is recorded at signature. A relier reads them without asking anyone.
Art. 8 · no modification after signing; changes by a separate complementary opinion
Nothing is edited after signing. A refreshed opinion is a new credential, a re-issue with its own date, and the old one is left to expire or is revoked.
Art. 11 · the white paper annexed as the version the opinion covers
The scope hash binds the exact document set. If a document changes, the hash stops matching, and the second step of the check fails.
Art. 17 · a clear security or not-security conclusion, not linguistically ambiguous
The conclusion lives in the opinion body, off-chain, encrypted and gated to named reliers. No score, rating or verdict goes on-chain.
Art. 19 · the issuer or the firm authorises KuCoin to use the opinion, or places no restriction on third-party reliance
Reliance mode and the named-relier list say who may rely; a reliance record shows that the relier accepted the terms, on a date.

The firm-tier and headcount tests have no on-chain equivalent by design: whether a firm clears a relier's bar is the relier's own call.

Opinion practice

What opinion practice says about reliance and dates.

A closing opinion speaks as of its date.American Bar Association, Business Law Section, Core Opinion Principles, section 4.1 (2017)

The Core Opinion Principles go on: the opinion giver has no obligation to update a closing opinion for later events or legal developments (section 4.1), and the opinion may be relied on only by its addressee and any other person expressly authorised to rely (section 4.2). A recipient is also not entitled to rely on an opinion it knows to be incorrect (section 1.4). These are 2017 principles, and the practice behind them is older.

A 2021 article in the American Bar Association's Real Property, Trust and Estate Law Section journal Opinions Matters describes how firms handle the parties who were not addressed. Unless the letter says otherwise, only the recipient named in it may rely. Where a firm allows a later party to rely, it generally limits that party to the rights the original addressee had, and only as of the original date. Recipients often ask that an opinion may be delivered to a party without that party being entitled to rely on it; reliance matters to the giver because it generally creates a duty of care.

For a listing team this means three things. A PDF that looks current is only dated; its date is the fact. A reliance letter is a second negotiation, and it may come back limited to the original date or marked as delivered but not to be relied upon. And a bring-down is a new letter with a new date, which restarts the same checks on the same file.

Dubai · VARA

What a VARA-licensed VASP keeps on file.

VARA (Dubai, outside DIFC) licenses VASPs per activity under rulebooks. Part VIII of the Market Conduct Rulebook (version of 19 May 2025) requires every VASP to set standards for the virtual assets it services, to run due diligence before and at all times during the activity, to publish those standards on its website, to keep the assessment records for eight years, and to notify VARA as soon as possible when an asset no longer meets the standards. The factors include securities-law exposure, any regulatory approvals the asset holds, and the issuer's background. One published example: CoinMENA's VA Standards (undated) commit the exchange to understand and document the regulatory treatment of each asset by VARA and other relevant authorities.

Part IV of the Broker-Dealer Services Rulebook (same version) is the heavier file, and it belongs to the Licensed Distributor, the VASP through which a Category 2 issuance is placed. The distributor must ensure the quality of each issuer and asset beyond all reasonable doubt (Rule IV.B.1), run comprehensive due diligence on both (IV.B.3 and IV.B.4), and submit to VARA the white paper, the risk disclosure statement, a signed compliance declaration and its due-diligence reports (IV.D.1), after which a fifteen Working Day Submission Period runs (IV.D.2). VARA is never deemed to have approved an asset by virtue of that submission (IV.D.7). Two rules speak directly to staleness: the diligence must stay up to date and accurate at all times (IV.E.1), and on any material change the distributor must redo it and resubmit (IV.E.3).

Note what the Category 2 package does not contain. VARA does not require a legal opinion in it; it requires comprehensive audits of all smart contracts used for the asset, with findings remediated (IV.B.3(e)). The one VARA text that names who may sign a legal opinion is narrower still: its Guidance on the Virtual Assets Issuance Rulebook (9 April 2026) covers an asset-referenced virtual asset issuance under Annex 2, and there it expects the issuer to keep that opinion current and to supply a revised or supplemental opinion after a material change. A co-signed opinion with an as-of date, a scope hash and a Live, Expired or Revoked status is evidence a distributor can file and re-check under IV.E.1 and IV.E.3. VARA does not ask for it.

A LEXI credential is a co-signed legal opinion committed on-chain. It is not a licence, a registration, or an approval by any regulator. LEXI is not licensed or endorsed by VARA.

Clearing a jurisdiction, in LEXI's vocabulary, means obtaining a co-signed legal opinion covering that jurisdiction. The VARA track as a whole is on VARA compliance (Dubai); the regulator events since 2024, each with what it does to an opinion's scope or date, are in the corridor tracker.

EU · MiCA · V1, next up

What the EU asks a platform to do before admission.

The EU (MiCA) is V1, next up, for LEXI; nothing below is live coverage. It is here because a listing team at an EEA venue runs a parallel check, and MiCA puts the gatekeeping on the platform rather than on an opinion. Before admitting a crypto-asset to trading, a platform operator must assess the suitability of the asset (Article 76(2)), including the reliability of its technology and any link to illicit or fraudulent activity, taking into account the experience, track record and reputation of the issuer and its development team. For an ordinary crypto-asset, the white paper notification carries an explanation of why the asset is not an excluded asset, an e-money token or an asset-referenced token (Article 8(4)); that is an explanation, not a legal opinion, and the regulation does not say who writes it. MiCA asks for a legal opinion only for asset-referenced tokens (Articles 17(1)(b)(ii) and 18(2)(e)).

Two public lists matter to a relier on this side of the corridor: ESMA's interim MiCA register of authorised crypto-asset service providers, and its register of non-compliant entities. Both move weekly, so any check records its snapshot date; the snapshot read for this briefing was 7 October 2026. Kraken's EEA rule, above, shows how a venue turns Article 76 into a file. When V1 reaches MiCA, the credential's as-of date is what a relier compares with the register row and with the white paper's notification date. Until then, this section describes a check other people run.

What changes

What a verifiable credential changes.

Three of the four questions above move out of the PDF and onto Base. Each step of the relier check reads one on-chain field, in one call, and reading the public metadata is free.

  1. Status is Live, not Expired or Revoked.

    As-of date, expiry and revocation are on-chain.Does it still speak as of a date I accept?

  2. The scope and opinion hashes match the documents you hold.

    Hash the PDF and the scope text and compare them with the on-chain commitments.Is this the document the lawyer signed?

  3. Your entity is a named relier and has accepted the reliance terms.

    The credential names who may rely on it; the reliance record shows you accepted the terms.Am I expressly authorised to rely?

The three-step relier check in full, the field table and a specimen are on the credential page. The relier page, for exchanges, VASPs and Licensed Distributors, maps a compliance file onto the credential item by item.

What you still do yourself

  • Read the scope. The scope text, the assumptions and reservations and the permitted-use terms are public off-chain. The credential records that an opinion exists and what it binds; what it concludes is in the body, for named reliers.
  • Treat Live as dated, not current. Live means unexpired and unrevoked. The as-of date and the regulator events after it, listed in the corridor tracker, tell you whether the opinion still fits the facts.
  • Check the signer. The signatory and firm ID are public fields. For a Dubai-registered signatory, the Government of Dubai Legal Affairs Department publishes a public directory of advocates and legal consultants (opens in a new tab) showing status, registration number and licence issue and expiry dates. It lists lawyers registered with the Dubai department; for a signatory registered elsewhere, ask for the registration certificate.
  • Keep your own file. Your VA Standards assessment, your suitability assessment or your listing committee's record stays yours. A credential is one dated, verifiable input to it, not a substitute for it.
  • Rely on nothing from testnet. V0 credentials exist on Base Sepolia (testnet) only. No reliance attaches to any testnet credential.

Questions

Questions listing teams ask.

Do exchanges require a legal opinion?

Not as a rule. Among the listing pages opened at source on 11 October 2026, only KuCoin publishes a written legal-opinion requirement. Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini Singapore, Bitget, Crypto.com, CoinDCX and Gate US describe an internal legal review and keep their own file; Kraken (for the EEA) and Bybit ask for a MiCA white paper. OKX and HTX publish no requirement that could be found; MEXC's own listing guide lists a legal opinion letter as optional, 'if available'; and Binance's application sits behind a login. Some listing teams require an opinion in writing; most run an internal legal review and keep a file.

What makes a legal opinion relied on?

Express authorisation. Under opinion practice a closing opinion may be relied on only by its addressee and any other person the firm expressly authorises, and it speaks as of its date, with no duty on the giver to update it. A party outside the addressee line asks for a reliance letter, which may be refused, limited to the original date, or marked as delivered but not to be relied upon. On a LEXI credential the named-relier list, the reliance mode and the on-chain reliance record carry that authorisation, and the as-of date and expiry carry the date.

How does a LEXI credential change the check?

It moves three facts out of the PDF and onto Base: whether the opinion is still live, whether the document set has changed since signing, and whether your entity may rely. A named relier checks that the status is Live, not Expired or Revoked; that the scope and opinion hashes match the documents it holds; and that its entity is a named relier that has accepted the reliance terms. Reading the public on-chain metadata is free; reliance access is a fixed USDC fee. V0 runs on Base Sepolia testnet, nothing is live on mainnet, and no reliance attaches to any testnet credential.
